Section 1: Your Paycheck

Learn

What Is Gross Pay?

Gross pay is the total amount your employer agrees to pay you before any deductions. If you make $16/hour and work 40 hours a week, your gross weekly pay is $640. Over a year (52 weeks), that is $33,280. This is the number on a job posting, but it is NOT the amount that shows up in your bank account.

What Is Net Pay?

Net pay (take-home pay) is what actually hits your bank account after all deductions. The gap between gross and net can be 20-35% of your paycheck. This is the only number that matters for budgeting because you cannot spend money you never received.

The Three Mandatory Deductions

1. Federal Income Tax uses a progressive system: income is taxed in layers. The first $11,600 is taxed at 10%. Income from $11,601-$47,150 at 12%. And so on. Only dollars above each threshold get the higher rate.

2. Arizona State Tax is a flat 2.5% on all earned income. Every dollar taxed at the same rate.

3. FICA is 7.65% split between Social Security (6.2%) for retirement/disability and Medicare (1.45%) for health coverage at 65+. Your employer matches it.

Section 2: Banking

Learn

Checking Accounts

Your everyday money hub. Paycheck lands here via direct deposit. You spend from it with a debit card or bill pay. Pays little to no interest (0.01% typical). The tradeoff: your money is always accessible but not growing.

Savings Accounts

Where you park money you do not need today. Online banks offer 4-5% APY. Traditional banks offer 0.01-0.50%. $1,000 in an online savings earns $40-50/year automatically. Some accounts limit withdrawals to 6/month.

FDIC Insurance

The FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor per bank. If your bank fails, the government guarantees your money. Covers checking, savings, CDs, and money market accounts.

Banking Fees

Overdraft ($35 each): Spending more than your balance. Three in a day = $105. Fix: opt OUT of overdraft protection.

Monthly maintenance ($5-$15): $12/month = $144/year. Fix: direct deposit waiver or online bank.

ATM fees ($3-$5): Using another bank's ATM. Twice a week = $300-500/year. Fix: use your own bank's ATMs.

Simple Interest Formula

Interest = Principal x Rate x Time

Example: $2,000 at 4.5% for 1 year = $2,000 x 0.045 x 1 = $90.

Section 3: Insurance

Learn

Why Insurance Exists

Some costs are too large for one person. A car accident: $30,000. A hospital stay: $100,000. Insurance works through risk pooling: thousands pay small premiums into a shared pool. Most months, most people do not claim. The pool covers the few who do.

Key Terms

Premium: Monthly payment whether or not you use it. Deductible: You pay first before insurance kicks in. Copay: Fixed amount per visit. Coinsurance: After deductible, you split costs (you 20%, insurer 80%). OOP Max: Most you pay in a year. After this, insurance covers 100%.

The Deductible Tradeoff

Higher deductible = lower premium but more risk. Lower deductible = higher premium but less risk. Choose based on your savings.

Section 4: Credit Cards

Learn

How Credit Cards Work

Pay the full balance by due date = zero interest (grace period). Carry a balance = interest at APR (20-30% typical).

The Minimum Payment Trap

Minimum (about 2% or $25) is set low on purpose. Most goes to interest, not balance. $2,000 at 25% APR takes 10+ years at minimums.

Credit Score (300-850)

1. Payment history (35%): Pay on time.

2. Utilization (30%): Keep under 30% of limit.

3. Length (15%): Longer = better.

4. Mix (10%): Different types help.

5. Inquiries (10%): Many apps = risky.

Section 5: Loans and Debt

Learn

Loan Terms

Principal: Amount borrowed. APR: Annual cost. Term: Repayment length. Longer term = lower payment but more total interest. The car costs the same either way.

Avalanche vs Snowball

Avalanche: Extra to highest APR first. Saves the most. Snowball: Extra to smallest balance first. Faster wins. Both work.

Identity Theft

Check your credit report yearly (annualcreditreport.com). Freeze credit at all three bureaus (free). Use unique passwords + two-factor auth. Never share SSN unless required.

Section 6: Your Budget

Take-home: $0/mo

Learn

The 50/30/20 Rule

50% needs (rent, groceries, insurance, transport, minimum debt). 30% wants (eating out, subs, entertainment). 20% savings + extra debt payoff.

Needs vs Wants

Needs cannot be eliminated without serious consequences. Wants are everything else. A phone is a need; a $60 unlimited plan might be a want if $25 prepaid works.

When It Does Not Balance

Cut wants first. Then reduce needs. Last resort: more income. An unbalanced budget is information, not failure.
